That sounds like a setting that’s well suited to the kind of writing you describe. The temptation on a two-week trip is to either “work” or “switch off.” Given your themes—liminality, otherness, relational ontology—it might be more fruitful to treat the holiday itself as a field study. Rather than trying to produce finished songs every day, create the conditions where songs can emerge.

Here’s one possible rhythm.

## The first three days: become porous

Don’t try to write songs immediately.

Instead:

- Swim every morning without headphones.
- Walk somewhere unfamiliar each day, taking a different route.
- Keep a notebook with only observations—not interpretations.
- Record voice notes whenever an image, phrase or question arrives.

Collect things like:

- Shadows crossing shutters.
- Conversations you only half understand.
- The feeling of moving between English and French.
- The sound of insects becoming louder than people.
- The smell of chlorine mixed with lavender.
- The threshold between indoors and outdoors.

Since your work explores liminality, ask yourself:

Where are the thresholds here?

Not metaphorically at first. Literally.

Doorways.  
Pool edges.  
Garden gates.  
Twilight.  
Markets before opening.  
The silence after everyone goes to bed.

### Middle week: begin conversations

Each afternoon choose one prompt.

For example:

Otherness

Write from the perspective of:

- the pool
- an empty chair
- a wasp
- someone who will arrive after you’ve left
- a forgotten object in the house

Not because anthropomorphism is clever, but because relational ontology asks us to loosen the human centre.

  

Relational ontology

Instead of asking

What do I feel?

ask

What becomes possible because these two things meet?

Examples:

- skin + water
- heat + stone
- stranger + language
- absence + memory
- child + echo
- tree + wind

Songs can emerge from relationships rather than characters.

  

Liminality

Spend twenty minutes each evening outside during blue hour.

Don’t write.

Just notice transitions.

Many songs fail because they’re about conclusions.

Your themes live inside transitions.

  

Creative constraints

Give yourself tiny rules.

For example:

- Every song begins with a place.
- Never explain the metaphor.
- Use one overheard French word.
- End before resolving.
- Write only in questions.
- Remove the chorus.
- Keep the first take.

Constraints often produce stranger, more authentic work than inspiration alone.

  

The pool as studio

Pools are fascinating liminal spaces.

They’re:

- neither land nor sea
- public yet private
- controlled nature
- mirrors that distort
- places where voices sound different

Spend one hour floating.

No phone.

Afterwards immediately write whatever language appears.

Don’t judge it.
### One “artist’s day”

Choose one day with almost no agenda.

Leave after breakfast.

Go to a village you’ve never heard of.

Don’t research it.

Buy a notebook in French.

Sit somewhere for two hours.

Write longhand.

Come home without feeling obliged to have “achieved” anything.

Unexpected places often produce more original work than famous destinations.

  

Evening practice

Each night answer only these four questions:

1. What surprised me?
2. What remained unresolved?
3. What relationship changed today?
4. Which image refuses to leave?

Those four answers become your lyric bank.

  

Read very little

Rather than consuming lots of new material, perhaps bring one or two books that resonate with your concerns and dip into them slowly. For example, The Spell of the Sensuous for its attention to perception and more-than-human relations, or Braiding Sweetgrass if you haven’t already spent time with it. Read a few pages, then go outside and let the landscape answer back.

  

Leave with fragments, not finished songs

A successful fortnight might not end with ten polished tracks.

It might end with:

- 80 observations.
- 40 voice notes.
- 25 images.
- 10 melodic ideas.
- 5 lyric fragments.
- 2 songs beginning to reveal themselves.

That kind of material often matures into stronger work once you’re home.

One thought keeps returning as I imagine your setting: a swimming pool is a human-made threshold between bodies and landscape. It is neither wilderness nor domestic interior; it is a carefully bounded piece of transformed nature. Given your interest in liminality and relational ontology, it could become more than a place to relax—it could become the central metaphor of the whole trip, a space where distinctions between self and environment, visitor and host, language and silence, are continually negotiated. If you return having understood that place a little more deeply, the songs may already have begun.
