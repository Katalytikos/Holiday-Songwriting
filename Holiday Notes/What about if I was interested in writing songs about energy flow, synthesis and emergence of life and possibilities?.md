I think that opens up an even richer direction. Rather than writing about emergence, you could write songs that behave like emergence: where simple motifs gradually combine into something neither predictable nor reducible to their parts.

That shifts the songwriting question from “What do I want to say?” to “What kind of system do I want to create?”

Spend two weeks following energy

Instead of collecting stories, collect movements.

Every day ask:

- Where did energy gather?
- Where did it dissipate?
- What transformed one thing into another?
- What new possibility emerged because of an interaction?

The answers might look like:

Wind + pine trees = a new rhythm.

Two strangers laughing = a temporary social organism.

Sunlight hitting the pool = a second sky.

Ants finding dropped fruit = an economy appears.

Those are all songs waiting to happen.

  

Think in verbs instead of nouns

Rather than writing about:

- love
- identity
- loneliness
- hope

Write about:

- converging
- folding
- carrying
- transmitting
- exchanging
- dissolving
- becoming
- resonating

Life isn’t made of objects.

It’s made of processes.

That immediately aligns your songwriting with emergence.

  

The house becomes an ecosystem

Instead of seeing the villa as a backdrop, imagine it as a living network.

Notice:

Morning:

- light enters rooms
- insects wake
- coffee begins circulating
- people disperse

Afternoon:

- heat pools
- conversation slows
- water absorbs energy
- birds disappear

Evening:

- temperature equalises
- smells travel further
- voices become quieter
- bats replace swallows

Nothing is static.

Everything is energy reorganising itself.

A song could simply trace those transitions.

  

Write from the perspective of systems

Instead of “I…”

Try:

We are the heat exchanging places.

or

This afternoon assembled itself from chlorine, dust, insects and waiting.

Or:

Nobody arrived.

The afternoon became us instead.

The self becomes something emergent rather than fixed.

  

Watch for synthesis

Every evening choose two things that don’t obviously belong together.

For example:

- moss + Wi-Fi
- cicadas + memory
- swimming pools + galaxies
- olive trees + lungs
- stone walls + sleep

Ask:

What third thing appears when these meet?

That “third thing” is often the heart of a song.

Not A.

Not B.

The relation.

  

Compose like evolution

Instead of writing verse–chorus–verse.

Try this:

Start with one line.

Repeat it with tiny mutations.

For example:

Water remembers nothing.

↓

Water remembers the light.

↓

Light remembers the swimmer.

↓

The swimmer remembers becoming water.

Each line evolves from the previous one.

The song itself becomes an emergent organism.

  

A daily practice: “One Emergence”

Spend 20 minutes looking for one thing that didn’t exist an hour earlier.

Examples:

- A shadow stretching across the pool.
- A conversation beginning.
- A cloud changing the colour of the garden.
- Ripples intersecting.
- Bread becoming toast.
- A melody appearing because two birds overlap.

Write only about that.

  

Read the landscape as metabolism

Imagine the countryside isn’t scenery.

It’s metabolism.

Energy flows:

Sun  
→ vines  
→ grapes  
→ wine  
→ people  
→ stories  
→ songs

Or

Sun  
→ pool  
→ evaporation  
→ clouds  
→ rain  
→ rivers

Nothing exists alone.

Everything is borrowing energy.

That’s profoundly lyrical.

Divide your notebook into four columns:

|   |   |   |   |
|---|---|---|---|
|Flow|Transformation|Relation|Emergence|
|What moved?|What changed?|What met?|What appeared?|

You might notice:

| Heat | Stone warms | Bare feet + stone | A slower walk |

After two weeks you’ll probably have dozens of these tiny “field notes.”

  

One guiding thought

Many songs begin with a feeling.

Your songs could begin with a process.

Instead of saying:

“I feel alone.”

you might ask:

“What patterns of relation produce aloneness? What new possibilities appear when those patterns shift?”

Or instead of writing about love as an emotion, write about it as a dynamic system:

Two lives exchange enough energy that each begins to organise differently.

That kind of writing has a quiet philosophical depth without becoming abstract, because it’s always grounded in observable transformations.

I also wonder whether Angoulême itself offers a beautiful metaphor for this approach. You’re there temporarily, passing through a place that has its own long history and rhythms. Rather than treating yourself as a visitor observing a landscape, you could imagine yourself as one more current flowing through an already-living system. The songs then aren’t “about France”; they’re records of what became possible because you and that place briefly interacted. That’s very close to both emergence and relational ontology: neither you nor the place is the whole story—the song is what arises between them.
